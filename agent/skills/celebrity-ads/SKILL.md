# Celebrity deepfake ads playbook

## Recognize the pattern

Treat a celebrity, doctor, presenter, or public figure in an advertisement as a
claim of endorsement, not proof of endorsement. Watch for a familiar face or
voice paired with miracle health claims, guaranteed returns, a limited-time offer,
or a link to an unfamiliar seller.

Tag `authority` when the ad borrows a person's reputation, `urgency` for a rush to
act, `scarcity` for limited availability, and `social_proof` for testimonials or
success stories presented as proof. Use deepfake language only when supported by
the evidence; an unverified endorsement can be misleading without being synthetic.

## Verification steps

1. Check the person's verified or official channels for the alleged endorsement.
2. Check the seller, product, and offer through independent sources rather than
   the ad's link, QR code, or contact details.
3. For health claims, look for an appropriate health authority or qualified
   professional. For investment claims, do not transfer money based on a video ad.
4. Do not download an app, disclose credentials, or make a payment to "claim" an
   offer before independent verification.

## Explanation boundary

Do not say a public figure approved an offer unless there is attributable evidence.
Do not say the ad is definitely AI-generated without a reliable provenance or
forensic result. The practical conclusion can still be clear: a famous face is not
evidence that an offer is genuine.
